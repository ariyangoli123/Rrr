Rouleaux size stands in for the acute-phase proteins that make red cells stack: bigger stacks settle faster, which is what an inflamed sample does. Tilt is the Boycott effect — three degrees is enough to inflate a reading, which is why a real tube is levelled. The tube leans in the drawing, with the clear layer running up its raised wall and the sediment sliding down the lowered one.

What is being solved

Cell volume is conserved in a tube whose cross-section A(z) varies along its length, so the shape of the tube enters the physics: where the bore narrows downward the suspension concentrates and slows, where it widens the suspension is diluted and the boundary accelerates, and a throat throttles the whole column. Hindered settling follows Richardson–Zaki with a packing limit; rouleaux take a few minutes to form; the tube wall drags on the cells near it.

Any wall that is not vertical is a settling surface, and the model counts its horizontal projection whether it got that way by tilting the tube or by being a cone. The velocity arrows and tracers are the solved field; the two wall layers a tilted tube develops are drawn schematically, because a one-dimensional model resolves the axis and not the width. Solved with finite volumes and the exact Godunov flux — the same code as the Python package, checked against it case by case. For understanding and teaching, not for calibrating a clinical instrument.
